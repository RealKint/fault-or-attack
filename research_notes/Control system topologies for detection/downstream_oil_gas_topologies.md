## 1. Tank farm / bulk storage terminal topology

### Takeaway
A real terminal is many tanks fed by pipelines the site may not even control, with tanks filling and emptying to loading bays at the same time. Level data travels on a dedicated tank-gauging network (tank bus → tank hub → control-room data concentrator → DCS/SCADA/IT), and the overfill device is a separate instrument wired to the shutdown system. The single-tank model already has the "two independent sensors" idea; it lacks shared hydraulics, an inflow the operator cannot see, and the loading-automation layer that real attackers have hit.

### Cited Findings
- **[SNIPPET]** Emerson's Rosemount tank gauging design connects all measuring devices on a tank over a digital 2-wire "Tankbus" to a "tank hub", normally at the tank foot. From the hub a fieldbus, often Modbus, carries data to the control room — [Emerson Rosemount technical article](https://s1-live.emerson.com/documents/automation/article-rosemount-tank-gauging-system-safety-efficiency-technical-article-en-81202.pdf)
- **[SNIPPET]** Data concentrators in the control room feed DCS systems, other HMI systems and general IT systems. Links to office computers and SCADA/DCS can use an OPC server or Modbus — [Emerson Rosemount technical article](https://s1-live.emerson.com/documents/automation/article-rosemount-tank-gauging-system-safety-efficiency-technical-article-en-81202.pdf)
- **[SNIPPET]** A separate radar gauge can provide the high-high alarm through SIL 2 or SIL 3 rated relay outputs that go to the emergency shutdown (ESD) system, independent of the primary gauge. A 4–20 mA HART output can alternatively be wired directly to the safety instrumented system — [Emerson Rosemount technical article](https://s1-live.emerson.com/documents/automation/article-rosemount-tank-gauging-system-safety-efficiency-technical-article-en-81202.pdf)
- **[SNIPPET]** The Rosemount 2410 tank hub supports Modbus, IEC 62591 (WirelessHART) and emulation of other vendors' protocols for control-room communication, and has two SIL-certified relays — [Applied Control product page](https://www.appliedcontrol.com/products/measurement-instrumentation/industrial-wireless/wireless-tank-gauging/rosemount-2410-tank-hub)
- **[READ]** Buncefield's Tank 912 held 6 million litres and had an automatic tank gauging (ATG) system that displayed the level on a control-room screen. A servo gauge works by a displacer following the liquid surface, and this one stuck — [HSE/COMAH, "Buncefield: Why did it happen?" (2011), hosted by IChemE](https://www.icheme.org/media/10706/buncefield-report.pdf)
- **[READ]** At Buncefield the site received fuel from three pipelines. Site supervisors controlled one (Finaline). The two UKOP lines were controlled from elsewhere, and supervisors had no SCADA view of whether those lines were online or their flow rate. Shortly before the explosion the UKOP South flow rate changed from 550 m3/hr to 900 m3/hr without the supervisors knowing — [HSE/COMAH Buncefield report](https://www.icheme.org/media/10706/buncefield-report.pdf)
- **[READ]** The report notes that inferring inflow from tank fill speed "was not an easy task" because a tank could be filling from the pipeline while feeding tanker bays, and more than one tank could be filling at once — [HSE/COMAH Buncefield report](https://www.icheme.org/media/10706/buncefield-report.pdf)
- **[SNIPPET]** Terminal cyber incident (2022): on 29 January 2022 Oiltanking GmbH Group and Mabanaft discovered a cyber incident affecting their IT systems. Oiltanking Deutschland operates 13 tank farms in Germany, which ran at limited capacity, and force majeure was declared — [SecurityAffairs](https://securityaffairs.com/127493/hacking/oiltanking-gmbh-cyber-attack.html), [GovInfoSecurity](https://www.govinfosecurity.com/cyberattack-cripples-european-oil-port-terminals-a-18465)
- **[SNIPPET]** Reports said the loading and unloading process was fully computerised and could not fall back to manual operation; Der Spiegel said truck operations were possible only to a "limited extent". The companies themselves said only "limited capacity", so severity is disputed between sources — [Computer Weekly](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/252512758/German-fuel-supplier-taken-offline-in-cyber-attack), [Techzine](https://www.techzine.eu/news/security/72286/german-fuel-and-oil-distributors-disrupted-by-cyberattack/)
- **[SNIPPET]** Reuters reported tanker and barge shipments at the Amsterdam-Rotterdam-Antwerp hub delayed by up to a week as four storage companies recovered from cyber attacks in the same period — [Tank Terminals, citing Reuters](https://tankterminals.com/news/oil-shipments-in-european-oil-hub-delayed-after-cyber-attacks/)

### Inferences
- **Idea A: tank farm with a shared manifold.** Model 2–3 tanks on one inlet header with motorised valves and one transfer pump. A real misrouting fault (stuck or passing valve) moves product between tanks, so the site-wide mass balance still closes. A spoofed level reading on one tank breaks the balance (metered in minus metered out no longer equals the sum of level changes). This is the strongest and easiest-to-explain fault-vs-attack discriminator.
- **Idea B: an inflow the operator cannot see.** Buncefield shows a real terminal can receive from a pipeline controlled by another party. Add an "external pipeline" mode where inflow changes without a local command (550 → 900 m3/hr is a ready-made test case). A detector that treats every unexplained inflow change as an attack would be wrong; it must check whether the level response is physically consistent with an inflow meter.
- **Idea C: simultaneous fill and draw.** Let the outlet (loading bay) run while the tank fills. The report says this makes level-from-flow inference hard for humans, which is exactly where an automated mass-balance detector adds value.
- **Idea D: gauging gateway.** Route sensor A through "tank bus → tank hub → data concentrator → DCS". A hub or concentrator compromise changes several tanks' readings at once with shared timing; a physical gauge fault affects one tank. Keep sensor B / the overfill switch on a relay path that bypasses this network.
- **Idea E: loading automation (TAS) node on business IT.** The Oiltanking case suggests a scenario where loading stops because IT is down while the tank physics is normal. Correct verdict: "IT-side cyber incident, process healthy".

### Gaps
- Could not open the Emerson PDF (it returned empty text) or the Port Technology paper (HTTP 403), so the Emerson architecture points are snippet-level only and come from one vendor.
- No source was found describing a Terminal Automation System architecture in detail. **Unverified background:** a TAS typically handles driver/vehicle ID, bay allocation, preset batch controllers and meters on loading arms, truck earthing and overfill interlocks, bills of lading, and stock reconciliation against tank gauging, and it links to ERP. Confirm with a vendor document before using.
- **Unverified background:** Honeywell Enraf and Endress+Hauser offer comparable servo/radar gauging systems; product names were not checked.

## 2. Layers of protection: BPCS vs SIS vs independent overfill (API 2350, IEC 61511, LOPA) and Buncefield

### Takeaway
Buncefield (11 December 2005) is a near-perfect real-world analogue of the project's model: a gauge on the control-room system flatlined, and the independent high-level switch that should have closed the inlet valves was silently inoperable, so no alarm fired at all. No attacker was involved. The lesson the investigators drew is that overfill protection must be independent of normal monitoring and must not rely on operators responding to alarms.

### Cited Findings
- **[READ]** Petrol delivery into Tank 912 began at 18:50 on Saturday 10 December 2005. At 03:05 on 11 December the ATG display "flatlined": it stopped registering the rising level while the tank kept filling — [HSE/COMAH Buncefield report](https://www.icheme.org/media/10706/buncefield-report.pdf)
- **[READ]** Because the displayed level stayed below the setpoints, none of the three ATG alarms ("user level", "high level", "high-high level") could operate — [HSE/COMAH Buncefield report](https://www.icheme.org/media/10706/buncefield-report.pdf)
- **[READ]** The tank also had an independent high-level switch (IHLS) set above the ATG alarms, intended to close valves on importing pipelines automatically and sound an audible alarm. It also failed, so the automatic shutdown did not activate — [HSE/COMAH Buncefield report](https://www.icheme.org/media/10706/buncefield-report.pdf)
- **[READ]** By 05:37 the tank exceeded its capacity and petrol spilled from roof vents. The vapour cloud spread to about 360 metres in diameter. The fire alarm was pressed at 06:01 and the explosion followed almost immediately. Over 250,000 litres had escaped. There were no fatalities and over 40 people were injured; the fire engulfed over 20 tanks — [HSE/COMAH Buncefield report](https://www.icheme.org/media/10706/buncefield-report.pdf)
- **[READ]** The servo gauge had stuck 14 times between 31 August 2005 (return to service after maintenance) and 11 December 2005. Supervisors sometimes freed it by "stowing" (raising the gauge to the top and letting it settle). The fault was sometimes logged and sometimes not, and the root cause was never identified — [HSE/COMAH Buncefield report](https://www.icheme.org/media/10706/buncefield-report.pdf)
- **[READ]** The IHLS was fitted on 1 July 2004. Its test lever needed a padlock to hold it in the working position. Installers thought the padlock was only for anti-tamper security and did not fit it, so after periodic tests the lever was left in, or could fall into, the inoperable position. Nobody on site knew the padlock mattered — [HSE/COMAH Buncefield report](https://www.icheme.org/media/10706/buncefield-report.pdf)
- **[READ]** The ATG had three high alarms: "user high" (set by the supervisor), "high" (below maximum working level) and "high-high" (below the IHLS trip point). The eight supervisors each used them differently, sometimes filling past "high" and occasionally past "high-high", and relied on alarms to control filling — [HSE/COMAH Buncefield report](https://www.icheme.org/media/10706/buncefield-report.pdf)
- **[READ]** The report's lesson, echoing the Baker report on Texas City (March 2005): protection systems "should not rely on operator response to alarms" and overfill protection should be independent of normal operational monitoring — [HSE/COMAH Buncefield report](https://www.icheme.org/media/10706/buncefield-report.pdf)
- **[READ]** API 2350 (as described by Emerson in October 2013; the page names no edition): Category 1 tanks are mainly manually operated with no transmitted alarms, at fully attended facilities. Category 2 requires an automatic tank gauging system that sends alarms to a remote location such as a control room. Category 3, the most common, requires a separate high-high level alarm. An Automated Overfill Prevention System (AOPS) sits outside the three categories and stops inflow automatically on high level without manual action — [Emerson Automation Experts, 2013](https://www.emersonautomationexperts.com/2013/measurement-instrumentation/api-2350-overfill-prevention-systems/)
- **[READ]** Same source: for Category 3 and AOPS tanks, the high-high sensor must be independent; two ATGs can serve as independent high-high alarms. Some sensors offer self-diagnostics for proof testing — [Emerson Automation Experts, 2013](https://www.emersonautomationexperts.com/2013/measurement-instrumentation/api-2350-overfill-prevention-systems/)
- **[SNIPPET]** API 2350 levels of concern, from the top: Critical High (CH), High-High (HH) alarm, Maximum Working (MW). Trade sources say the AOPS activation level must be at or above HH. Sources conflict on which alarms are mandatory, and one gives the 5th edition date as about 2018, another context implies 2020–2021 — [Inspenet](https://inspenet.com/en/articles/api-2350-for-tanks-overfill-prevention/), [Process Worldwide](https://www.process-worldwide.com/reducing-the-risk-of-overfillswhat-you-should-consider-now-a-f3e6e57af890592a3bee703829163984/)
- **[SNIPPET]** TRITON/TRISIS (2017): malware targeted Schneider Electric Triconex safety controllers at a petrochemical plant in Saudi Arabia. Schneider's analysis said the attacker needed access to the safety network and a workstation on it, and the controller's memory-protection key switch had to be in "PROGRAM" mode. Experts flagged the key switch being left in program mode during operation, and safety controllers being reachable from other networks. The campaign was reported discovered because an attacker mistake tripped the safety system — [Dark Reading](https://www.darkreading.com/vulnerabilities---threats/schneider-electric-triton-trisis-attack-used-0-day-flaw-in-its-safety-controller-system-and-a-rat/d/d-id/1330845), [Control Engineering](https://www.controleng.com/articles/advice-from-the-triton-cybersecurity-incident/)

### Inferences
- **Direct mapping to the project.** Sensor A on the control network = Buncefield's ATG. The full-level switch = the IHLS. Hard-wired sensor B = an API 2350 Category 3 independent high-high device. The pitch can say the model reproduces the Buncefield layering and that the detector covers the gap that existed there: nothing was watching for "level not moving while product is flowing in".
- **Idea F: a "Buncefield replay" scenario.** Gauge flatlines at a set time, the high switch is dormant, inflow continues at a metered rate. The detector should (1) flag the flatline by zero variance during a known transfer, (2) estimate true level from inflow minus outflow, (3) raise a predicted time-to-overfill (Buncefield allowed about 2.5 hours between flatline at 03:05 and spill at 05:37), and (4) label it a fault because the signal is frozen, the device has a sticking history, and no network anomaly exists.
- **Idea G: stuck vs spoofed.** A stuck gauge gives a constant value with no noise. A replay or injection attack gives a plausible, noisy trace that disagrees with the mass balance and with sensor B. Signal texture plus physics separates the two.
- **Idea H: instrument fault history.** The gauge stuck 14 times in about 3.5 months before the incident. Keep a per-instrument fault counter and "time since last proof test". A device with a history that fails again is likely a fault; a device with a clean history that suddenly disagrees with two independent references is more suspicious.
- **Idea I: dormant safety device.** Model the high switch as possibly disabled with no indication (padlock analogue). Add a periodic "proof test" action; a switch never seen to change state is flagged "unproven", which is a maintenance fault state, not an attack.
- **Idea J: SIS layer with ESD inlet valve and a mode flag.** Give the SIS its own logic that closes the inlet on sensor B / the switch regardless of the controller. Add a "program/bypass mode" flag (TRITON key-switch analogue). Bypass during a logged maintenance window and then forgotten = procedural fault. Mode change or setpoint change with no work order = attack indicator.
- **Idea K: alarm-setpoint tampering.** Since supervisors could set "user high" themselves, add scenarios where alarm setpoints are raised. With an operator log entry it is risky practice; without one it is tampering.
- **Idea L: protection-layer panel on the website.** Show layers (control alarm, operator response, independent high-high, SIS trip, bund) and which ones each scenario defeated. Layers failing at the same instant suggests a common cause such as an attack or power loss; layers that were already dormant suggests latent faults.

### Gaps
- The API 2350 standard text is paywalled and was not read. The exact edition in force in 2026, the 5th-edition date, required response times and which alarms are mandatory remain unconfirmed; the secondary sources conflict.
- **Unverified background (no source opened):** IEC 61511 requires the SIS to be separate from the BPCS and uses SIL 1–4; LOPA assigns each independent protection layer a probability of failure on demand, with a BPCS loop usually credited no better than a risk reduction of 10. Confirm against an IEC 61511 summary before using numbers on the website.
- **Unverified background:** the CAPECO tank farm explosion (Puerto Rico, 23 October 2009) was another overfill during a ship transfer with faulty gauging, investigated by the US Chemical Safety Board. Not checked this session.
- The UK Process Safety Leadership Group's post-Buncefield standards report (2009) was not read.
- TRITON details are from trade press snippets; accounts differ on whether the plant actually tripped.

## 3. Refinery / plant control topology (DCS, field buses, wireless, historians, remote access)

### Takeaway
This session found little directly sourced material on refinery DCS topology; the verified points are limited to segmentation guidance and one Brunei example. The standard picture (Purdue levels, redundant controllers and networks, HART/Fieldbus/Modbus/OPC UA, WirelessHART/ISA100, historian in a DMZ) is recorded below as unverified background.

### Cited Findings
- **[READ]** CISA/FBI guidance: organise OT assets into logical zones by criticality and consequence, define acceptable communication paths between zones, filter and monitor that traffic, keep ICS protocols off the IT network, and define a DMZ that eliminates unregulated IT–OT communication — [CISA Advisory AA21-131A (11 May 2021, revised 8 July 2021)](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa21-131a)
- **[READ]** Yokogawa recommends ISA/IEC 62443 and NIST guidance for SCADA security, passive (low-impact) intrusion detection so monitoring does not disturb the operating network, and traffic logging to establish baselines — [Yokogawa, "Cyber Security for Pipelines, Other SCADA Systems" (2017)](https://www.yokogawa.com/be-nl/library/resources/media-publications/cyber-security-for-pipelines-other-scada-systems/)
- **[SNIPPET]** A Brunei example of plant topology: Brunei Shell Petroleum's Champion West Phase III platform (2005–2006) used Emerson DeltaV with Foundation Fieldbus field devices, connected to third-party shutdown systems, multiphase meters and a DCS on another platform, and was unmanned and controlled remotely from an onshore centre — [Process Online](https://www.processonline.com.au/content/industrial-networks-buses/news/emerson-on-the-brunei-shell-petroleum-platform-676117807)
- **[SNIPPET]** TRITON lesson relevant to plant topology: the safety controllers were reachable from other operational networks instead of being isolated — [Control Engineering](https://www.controleng.com/articles/advice-from-the-triton-cybersecurity-incident/)

### Inferences
- **Idea M: draw the model as zones.** Field (sensors, valve, pump) / control (controller) / safety (SIS, hard-wired B and switches) / supervisory (HMI, detector) / DMZ (historian) / business IT. Each attack scenario states which zone boundary was crossed. The detector's passive, read-only position matches the "passive intrusion detection" recommendation.
- **Idea N: device status word.** Have sensor A send a status flag with its value (good / uncertain / device fault). Real faults often set a diagnostic flag; spoofed data usually arrives flagged "good" while physics disagrees. Cheap to add.
- **Idea O: redundant network paths and controller pair.** A cable or card fault shows as a clean failover on one path. Manipulated data shows as both paths agreeing with each other but disagreeing with the hard-wired references, or as timing/sequence anomalies.
- **Idea P: wireless sensor with link-quality metric.** Gradual signal-strength loss = environmental fault. Sudden loss on all wireless devices = jamming. Perfect link quality with implausible values = injection.
- **Idea Q: the BSP pattern.** An unmanned facility run remotely with a third-party shutdown system is a real Brunei precedent for modelling "remote operator + local independent safety layer".

### Gaps
- **Unverified background (no source opened this session):**
  - Purdue/ISA-95 levels 0–5 with an industrial DMZ between site operations and enterprise IT; IEC 62443 zones and conduits with security levels 1–4.
  - Refinery DCS products (Honeywell Experion with Fault Tolerant Ethernet, Yokogawa CENTUM VP with Vnet/IP, Emerson DeltaV) use redundant controller pairs and dual control networks.
  - HART carries digital diagnostics on top of 4–20 mA; Foundation Fieldbus H1 is an all-digital bus; classic Modbus has no authentication; OPC UA adds certificates and encryption.
  - WirelessHART (IEC 62591) and ISA100.11a (IEC 62734) are 2.4 GHz mesh networks with AES-128, used mostly for monitoring, not fast control or safety.
  - Historians usually sit in or above a DMZ and remote access goes through jump hosts.
- No vendor architecture document for a refinery DCS was opened. A follow-up task should fetch one (e.g., a Yokogawa or Honeywell system overview) to confirm the above.

## 4. Pipeline SCADA topology and Colonial Pipeline (2021)

### Takeaway
Pipeline stations each have an RTU that gathers field devices and reports over radio, satellite, cellular or fibre to a central control room with a backup centre; leak detection compares metered inflow and outflow and modelled pressures. Colonial Pipeline (May 2021) was an IT-network ransomware attack with no evidence of OT compromise, yet the operator stopped the pipeline for six days. The official lesson is to segment IT from OT and be able to keep OT running, including on manual control, when IT is down.

### Cited Findings
- **[READ]** At a remote site such as a pumping station, field instruments and actuators connect to an RTU that consolidates their data into one protocol (e.g., Modbus or proprietary). Local control may be done by the RTU or by a PLC reporting through it. The RTU uses a backhaul such as 900 MHz radio or satellite to reach the central control area — [Yokogawa (2017)](https://www.yokogawa.com/be-nl/library/resources/media-publications/cyber-security-for-pipelines-other-scada-systems/)
- **[READ]** Weak points: older RTUs are poorly defended with unsecured communications and are "the path of least resistance"; old 900 MHz radio links suffer interference and are often unreliable; vendor monitoring networks interconnected with the operator's SCADA can be a conduit; ransomware can lock out RTU access — [Yokogawa (2017)](https://www.yokogawa.com/be-nl/library/resources/media-publications/cyber-security-for-pipelines-other-scada-systems/)
- **[SNIPPET]** Pipelines combine satellite, radio, cellular, wired and fibre links, typically with automatic failover to a backup path. One vendor-oriented source gives VSAT as roughly 64–4096 kbps with 600–1200 ms latency. Vendor documents describe redundant servers and off-site backup control centres — [UTSI](https://utsi.com/2026/08/21/remote-monitoring-for-pipelines-using-scada/), [Industrial Monitor Direct](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/telecontrol-plc-options-for-pipeline-scada-systems), [ABB SCADAvantage](https://library.e.abb.com/public/135dbcbb44a3dbbdc1257b1a005b12ca/1328SCADAvantageL.pdf)
- **[SNIPPET]** Leak detection: mass balance (upstream metered volume vs downstream metered volume) is the most common method. Computational pipeline monitoring (CPM) compares measured pressure and flow with modelled values. A vendor blog quotes a typical 1% alarm threshold and 30–60 minute detection time; these figures are from a non-authoritative source — [Industrial Monitor Direct](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/oil-pipeline-instrumentation-sensors-esd-systems-and-leak-detection)
- **[SNIPPET]** API Std 1164 3rd edition, "Pipeline Control Systems Cybersecurity", was published 18 August 2021. It is based on the NIST Cybersecurity Framework and NERC-CIP, and widened scope from SCADA only to all pipeline control systems — [API news release](https://www.api.org/news-policy-and-issues/news/2021/08/18/third-edition-of-api-standard-1164)
- **[READ]** Colonial Pipeline: data was stolen on 6 May 2021 and ransomware struck on 7 May. Entry was through a compromised password for an inactive VPN account with no multi-factor authentication. The shutdown lasted six days; restart began 12 May and systems were normal by 15 May. The pipeline carries about 45% of East Coast fuel. Colonial paid 75 bitcoin (about USD 4.4 million); the DOJ later recovered 63.7 bitcoin. Nearly 100 GB of data was taken — [Wikipedia, Colonial Pipeline ransomware attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_Pipeline_ransomware_attack)
- **[READ]** CISA/FBI: DarkSide ransomware was deployed against the company's IT network, the company "proactively disconnected certain OT systems", and "there are no indications that the threat actor moved laterally to OT systems" — [CISA Advisory AA21-131A](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa21-131a)
- **[READ]** Reasons for the shutdown differ by source: a CNN source said the company could not bill customers; Colonial said it was a precaution. The same Wikipedia article also says the attack hit "computer equipment that manages the pipeline", which sits awkwardly with the CISA statement — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_Pipeline_ransomware_attack); compare [CISA AA21-131A](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa21-131a)
- **[READ]** CISA/FBI mitigations: robust IT/OT segmentation so a compromised IT network cannot be used to pivot to OT; identify IT–OT interdependencies and develop workarounds or manual controls; ensure OT can run at necessary capacity if IT is compromised; regularly test manual controls — [CISA AA21-131A](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa21-131a)
- **[READ]** TSA issued a security directive on 27 May 2021 requiring pipeline operators to report cyber incidents to CISA. **[SNIPPET]** A second directive in July 2021 required mitigation measures for IT and OT — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_Pipeline_ransomware_attack), [Dark Reading](https://www.darkreading.com/risk/dhs-orders-pipeline-operators-to-report-cyberattacks-review-security-posture/d/d-id/1341154)

### Inferences
- **Idea R: remote station RTU over a lossy link.** Put the inlet pump/valve at a "remote station" polled every N seconds over radio or satellite with latency (use the 600–1200 ms VSAT figure as a parameter after checking it) and packet loss. Fault: interference gives stale data and gaps with a comm-fail flag. Attack: data arrives fresh and complete but contradicts the hard-wired local sensors, or commands appear with no operator action. "Stale vs fresh-but-wrong" is the discriminator.
- **Idea S: leak vs falsified meter.** With an inlet meter at the station and an outlet meter at the tank, imbalance with a pressure drop means a physical leak. Imbalance with no pressure change points to a meter fault or falsified meter. This is a small CPM-style module.
- **Idea T: Colonial scenario.** Add a business-IT node (billing/scheduling). Ransomware takes it down while process readings stay normal. The detector's correct output is "cyber incident in IT, OT consistent with physics", and the dashboard shows whether the site can keep running on manual control. It teaches that a detector should say where the problem is, not only that one exists.
- **Idea U: failover visibility.** Give the remote link a primary and backup path. Legitimate failover is logged; an attacker forcing traffic onto a path they control looks like an unexplained path change.

### Gaps
- API RP 1130 itself was not read (paywalled); the leak detection points are from vendor blogs.
- Whether API 1164 aligns with IEC 62443 could not be confirmed from the sources found.
- The current TSA pipeline security directive revision in force in 2026 was not checked.
- The exact reason Colonial stopped the pipeline remains disputed between the company's statement and press reporting.

## 5. Brunei context

### Takeaway
Brunei has had a Cybersecurity Order since 2023 that lists "services relating to energy" first among essential services, lets a Commissioner of Cybersecurity designate critical information infrastructure (CII), and obliges CII owners to detect and report incidents, audit every two years and assess risk every year. No public information was found on the control systems or OT security practices of Brunei LNG or Hengyi, and only one dated example for Brunei Shell Petroleum.

### Cited Findings
- **[READ]** The Cybersecurity Order, 2023 was made on 20 May 2023 by His Majesty the Sultan under Article 83(3) of the Constitution. Its long title covers measures "to prevent, manage and respond to cybersecurity threats and incidents" and regulating CII owners. The responsible Minister is the Minister of Transport and Infocommunications — [Cybersecurity Order, 2023 (gazette text hosted by AIPA)](https://aipalync.org/document/view/1813)
- **[READ]** Schedule of essential services (10 items): energy; infocommunications; healthcare; banking and finance; defence and security; emergency services; aviation; functioning of Government; media; water — [Cybersecurity Order, 2023](https://aipalync.org/document/view/1813)
- **[READ]** Section 9: the Commissioner may designate a computer or computer system as CII if it is necessary for continuous delivery of an essential service, its loss or compromise would have a "debilitating effect" on that service, and it is located wholly or partly in Brunei — [Cybersecurity Order, 2023](https://aipalync.org/document/view/1813)
- **[READ]** Section 16: a CII owner must notify the Commissioner of prescribed cybersecurity incidents affecting the CII, and also those affecting any interconnected system under the owner's control, within a prescribed period. The owner must also establish mechanisms and processes to detect cybersecurity threats and incidents as set out in a code of practice. Failure to notify carries a fine up to $100,000, up to 2 years' imprisonment, or both — [Cybersecurity Order, 2023](https://aipalync.org/document/view/1813)
- **[READ]** Section 17: CII owners must have a compliance audit at least once every 2 years by an approved auditor and conduct a cybersecurity risk assessment at least once a year. Section 18 allows the Commissioner to run cybersecurity exercises — [Cybersecurity Order, 2023](https://aipalync.org/document/view/1813)
- **[SNIPPET]** Cyber Security Brunei (CSB) is the lead coordinating agency for policy, threat monitoring and incident management. ## 6. Summary: which topology upgrades make which faults and attacks distinguishable

### Takeaway
The most useful upgrades add independent references that are physically coupled to the tank: flow-based mass balance, a separately wired safety layer, and link/device health data. Across the cases read, faults show degraded or frozen data on one device with a history (Buncefield), while cyber incidents either leave the process healthy and hit IT (Colonial, Oiltanking) or target the safety layer's configuration (TRITON).

### Cited Findings
- **[READ]** Fault archetype: one gauge frozen, prior sticking history, dormant safety switch, no alarms — [HSE/COMAH Buncefield report](https://www.icheme.org/media/10706/buncefield-report.pdf)
- **[READ]** IT-attack archetype: IT network encrypted, no indication of lateral movement to OT, operator disconnects OT as a precaution — [CISA AA21-131A](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa21-131a)
- **[SNIPPET]** Safety-layer attack archetype: safety controller reachable from other networks with its key switch left in program mode — [Control Engineering](https://www.controleng.com/articles/advice-from-the-triton-cybersecurity-incident/)

### Inferences
| Upgrade (section) | Fault signature | Attack signature | Effort in Python sim + website |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buncefield replay scenario (2) | Gauge flatline, zero noise, switch dormant, inflow continues | — | Low |
| Mass balance from inlet/outlet meters (1, 4) | Leak: imbalance plus pressure drop | Spoofed level or meter: imbalance with no physical cause | Low |
| Multiple tanks on a shared manifold (1) | Stuck/passing valve: product moves between tanks, site balance closes | One tank's level disagrees with site balance | Medium |
| Externally controlled inflow (1) | Unannounced but physically consistent rate change | Rate reading changes with no matching level response | Low |
| Gauging gateway/tank hub (1) | One gauge misbehaves | Several gauges shift together | Low |
| SIS + ESD valve + bypass/program flag (2) | Bypass left on after logged maintenance; unproven switch | Mode or setpoint change with no work order | Medium |
| Instrument fault history and proof-test age (2) | Repeat offender fails again | Clean device suddenly contradicts two references | Low |
| Device status word (3) | Bad value with "fault/uncertain" status | Wrong value with "good" status | Low |
| Remote RTU over radio/satellite (4) | Stale data, gaps, comm-fail flag | Fresh, complete data contradicting hard-wired sensors; unsolicited commands | Medium |
| Business-IT/TAS node (1, 4) | — | IT ransomware with healthy OT; loading halted | Low |
| "Report to CSB?" verdict field (5) | Maintenance work order | Draft incident notification | Low |

Suggested build order for a hackathon: Buncefield replay → mass balance → device status word and fault history → SIS bypass flag → IT-down scenario → multi-tank manifold → remote RTU link.

### Gaps
- These discriminators are design reasoning from incident reports. They were not checked against published attack-detection research in this session; another researcher's notes may cover that.
- Thermal expansion as a benign cause of level change (a useful false-positive test) is unverified background and needs a source.
