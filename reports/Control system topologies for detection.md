# Give the lone tank independent witnesses

The "Fault or Attack" topology is sound in its core idea and thin everywhere else: it has one tank, one flat network, one detector and one mass-balance residual, so several different causes look the same to it. The research found about twenty topologies worth comparing, and they all improve ours in one of three ways. **Zoned networks** (Purdue levels, IEC 62443 zones and conduits, sensors tapped at more than one level) tell the detector *where* an attack entered, which a flat network cannot. **Coupled plants** (SWaT, the three-tank DTS200, C-Town, real tank farms) replace our single residual with a pattern of residuals, so a leak, a stuck valve and a spoofed sensor each leave a different signature. **Independent layers** (a safety system on its own wiring, a third model-based level channel, pressure sensors beside the flow meters) give the detector witnesses an attacker on the control network cannot reach. The best value for a hackathon comes from the changes that need no new plant: a Buncefield replay scenario, a third "virtual" level channel with a 2-out-of-3 vote, a meter-spoof rule in the pipeline game, a status word on sensor A, a setpoint-injection attack, and an IT-down scenario with a "report to CSB?" output. A zoned network view and a second tank with its own controller are the two medium-effort upgrades that add the most after that. The topology facts below are mostly checked against sources, but almost every "this would improve our detector" claim is the researchers' own design reasoning, and no source tested fault-versus-attack discrimination on a coupled-tank rig directly.

## Three grades of evidence sit behind this report

The four research notes used different tags, so this report merges them into three grades and uses them consistently. **Checked** means a researcher opened the cited page and read the passage in this project (the notes' [F], [READ], [V-full], [V] and unmarked items). **Secondary** means the claim comes from a search-result summary, an abstract, a vendor page or trade press, and the primary document was not opened ([S], [SNIPPET], [V-abs], "(secondary)"). **Inference** means the researchers' own design proposal: a way to apply a topology to our simulator, an effort estimate, or a ranking. Claims the researchers held only from memory are left out, or named as unverified where they matter to a build decision.

Four things should not be quoted as fact. The texts of IEC 62443, IEC 61511, API 2350 and API RP 1130 are paywalled and were not read, so everything about them here is secondary. The standard refinery DCS picture (redundant controllers, HART and Foundation Fieldbus, WirelessHART, historian in a DMZ) had no source opened and is unverified background. The pipeline pressure-wave location formula is from memory. All effort estimates and the final ranking are judgement, since no source ranks these upgrades against each other.

## A flat network cannot say where the attack came in

The Purdue model stacks a plant from sensors and actuators at level 0, through controllers at level 1 and HMIs at level 2, up to business networks at levels 4 and 5, with an industrial perimeter network between OT and IT ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/defender-for-iot/organizations/best-practices/understand-network-architecture)). Our model compresses levels 0 to 2, plus an attacker who really belongs on the internet, onto one wire. The difference matters for detection. CISA's alert on the 2015 Ukraine attack records that the attackers opened breakers through existing remote-administration tools over VPN, apparently with legitimate credentials ([CISA IR-ALERT-H-16-056-01](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/ics-alerts/ir-alert-h-16-056-01)). Our "writes from an unknown host" counter would stay silent in that case, because the host was a known one. The researchers' proposal is to give every host a level and every attack an entry level and a path, and to add setpoint-range and rate rules for commands that arrive from an authorised machine. That mapping is inference; the incident facts are checked.

IEC 62443 turns the same idea into zones joined by controlled conduits, with "restricted data flow" as one of seven foundational requirements ([IEC SyC Smart Energy](https://syc-se.iec.ch/deliveries/cybersecurity-guidelines/security-standards-and-best-practices/iec-62443/); [Trend Micro](https://www.trendmicro.com/en_us/research/20/g/guidelines-related-to-security-in-smart-factories-part-2-system-design-and-security-level-of-iec62443.html)). These are secondary sources, since the standard is paywalled. What was checked is the practical advice built on it: CISA recommends "monitoring-only access enforced by data diodes", and Mandiant recommends a one-way gateway for anything that needs safety-system data ([CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/ics-alerts/ir-alert-h-16-056-01); [Mandiant](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/attackers-deploy-new-ics-attack-framework-triton)). The honest finding for our project is that **conduits help with only half of our attack list**. Command injection and flooding from outside become events that are blocked or counted at the boundary. Replay, false-data injection and the stealth spoof start inside the control zone, and no firewall touches them. A "flat versus zoned" toggle on the website would show both halves: the same attack run on both layouts, with the outside attacks stopped at the conduit and the inside ones still needing physics and the watermark.

Where the detector listens also changes what it sees. OT network sensors are passive and receive mirrored traffic, and traffic that stays inside one segment never reaches a sensor placed at the gateway, so it must be captured at the access switch ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/defender-for-iot/organizations/best-practices/understand-network-architecture)). Our single detector, sitting with the controller and reading everything, is the most generous possible vantage point and no real plant has it. The researchers propose three detectors in its place: a tap on sensor A's field segment, a sensor at the supervisory boundary, and a physics monitor reading the hard-wired channel. Their visibility matrix then yields a localisation rule: **the highest-zone sensor that fires marks the entry level, and if only the physics monitor fires while every network sensor is clean, suspect a fault**, or a compromise below the network such as altered controller logic. No paper or dataset was found that backs this exact matrix, so present it as a design, not a result.

The same research corrects two things in the project's earlier material. The live MITRE ATT&CK for ICS matrix no longer lists T0855 and T0856 under their old names; the closest current entries are Unauthorized Message: Command Message (T1692.001) and Reporting Message (T1692.002), so any slide should cite the new IDs and note the old ones ([MITRE ATT&CK for ICS](https://attack.mitre.org/matrices/ics/)). And the Oldsmar water incident should not be shown as a confirmed hack: CISA says the access route "cannot be confirmed", and in 2023 a former city manager called it user error ([CISA AA21-042A](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa21-042a); [Industrial Cyber](https://industrialcyber.co/news/oldsmar-water-treatment-plant-incident-allegedly-caused-by-human-error-not-remote-access-cybersecurity-breach/)). That uncertainty makes it a real-world "fault or attack?" case, which suits the project better than a hack story would.

## One tank yields one residual; coupled tanks yield a pattern

The public testbeds differ from ours mainly in having more than one stage. SWaT is a six-stage plant with one PLC and one backup PLC per stage, an Ethernet ring from each PLC to its field devices, and a separate star network between PLCs ([Conti et al. survey](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2102.05631)). Its design-derived detection rules come in two kinds: one mass balance per tank, and state rules such as "valve open means flow is non-zero", some of which use conditions from neighbouring stages ([hackfest assessment paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1809.04786), secondary). Our mass-balance and actuator-feedback checks are already the single-tank versions of both. What we lack is the cross-stage kind, and that needs a second stage. Papers using the 2015 dataset count 36 attacks, of which 26 touch a single stage at a single point ([Kravchik and Shabtai](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.08110), secondary; iTrust's own page says 41). A second tank with its own controller forces an attacker out of that easy majority: a false level in tank 1 must also agree with tank 2's filling history and the transfer flow.

The fault-diagnosis benchmarks give the reason in structural terms. A single tank gives one mass-balance residual, and a leak, a biased meter, a drifting sensor and a spoofed level all move that same number. The DTS200 three-tank plant is a standard benchmark where structured residuals isolate sensor and actuator faults, and where the choice of measured outputs changes which faults can be isolated ([Theilliol et al. record](https://research.uaeu.ac.ae/en/publications/fault-diagnosis-and-accommodation-of-a-three-tank-system-based-on/); [arXiv 2207.13803](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.13803), both secondary). The researchers' argument for our case is that a real leak changes the flow into the next tank, so the neighbour sees it, while a spoofed level changes nothing physical, so the neighbour stays quiet. This is the notes' central inference, and they say plainly that it combines fault-isolation and attack-detection results from separate papers. No source tested it on one rig.

Two checked results show why extra signals matter. On BATADAL's C-Town data, replaying all sensors cut a detector's recall from 0.60 to 0, but **replay hid the attack only when at least 40 of the 43 sensors were controlled; replaying fewer raised detection**, because old and live readings contradicted each other ([Erba et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.07487)). With as few signals as our tank has, an attacker can replay everything. Each independent signal we add raises the share of the plant the attacker must own, and hard-wired sensor B is our one signal that cannot be replayed from the network. The second result is a warning. On a real quadruple-tank rig, a replay attack combined with water theft, and a slowly ramped bias, both stayed under the residual threshold and went undetected; a zero-dynamics attack emptied a tank in about 25 seconds before the alarm ([Teixeira et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1212.0226)). A model-based residual alone is not enough, which is exactly why our design carries sensor B, watermark probes and network counters. A demo that shows the residual missing a replay and sensor B catching it would reproduce a published finding.

The other testbeds contribute smaller, cheaper ideas. HAI pairs a demand tag with a position tag on its actuators and couples three vendors' control loops through simulated setpoints ([HAI repository](https://github.com/icsdataset/hai)), which suggests checking command against position on every actuator we have. The Morris water tank is close to ours, about two litres under on/off control on Modbus, with 28 attacks across two lab systems ([Morris and Gao](https://dl.ifip.org/IFIP-AICT-441/hal-01386754), secondary). The survey of its gas-pipeline data found setpoint injection among the hardest attacks to detect ([Conti et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2102.05631)). Our attack list has no equivalent: changing the 40/60 band leaves the plant behaving consistently around the wrong band, so every physics check passes and only a configuration-integrity check catches it. On Tennessee Eastman, monitoring controller-level and process-level data together separated disturbances from intrusions only "to a certain extent" ([Iturbe et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.01679)), which supports our decision to add network evidence and a second sensor.

One housekeeping item affects the project's existing validation claims. The HAI repository's table for version 22.04 lists four test files whose attack counts total 58, where the project's earlier note recorded 34, the sum of the first three files only ([HAI repository](https://github.com/icsdataset/hai)). The researcher flagged that the column headers need re-reading, so confirm them, then either restate the count as 58 or say that only three test files were used.

## Three level channels are the minimum to outvote one liar

Secure-estimation theory gives the one hard rule in this research. Fawzi, Tabuada and Diggavi prove that a decoder can correct q attacked sensors only if every state direction is seen by more than 2q sensors ([Fawzi et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1205.5073)). Applied to our tank, which has one state, the level, this means **surviving one spoofed channel needs at least three channels carrying level information**. Sensors A and B make two: they can disagree, but neither can say which is wrong. The third does not need hardware. A model-based "virtual level", integrated from actuator feedback and the outlet flow meter, supplies it, and a pairwise comparison then names the odd one out. If only A disagrees, A is faulty or spoofed; if only the model disagrees, the process itself has a problem such as a leak or a wrong meter. The theorem is checked; the application to our tank is inference, though a direct one. Urbina et al. add checked support for building the model as one correlated system: models that link several signals beat per-signal checks, and stateful CUSUM tests limit a stealthy attacker better than per-sample thresholds at the same false-alarm rate ([Urbina et al.](https://users.soe.ucsc.edu/~alacarde/papers/ccs16.pdf)).

Real plants get their third opinion from different sensor physics. Radar resists some failure causes of differential-pressure level measurement, such as a plugged impulse line, while foam and vapour trouble radar instead ([Chemical Engineering](https://chemengonline.com/safety-instrumented-systems-focus-on-measurement-diagnostics), secondary). For us this suggests a discrimination clue more than a build item: a physical fault follows one sensor's physics, while a network attack appears only on the networked channel. A deviation confined to sensor A, with B and the model in agreement, points to a cyber cause. The researchers flag the limit themselves. A careful attacker who spoofs only A and leaves the network counters clean looks exactly like a faulty sensor A, and that row of their decision table has no resolution. It should land in an "uncertain" verdict, not a confident one.

Topology also bounds what the watermark can do. Weerakkody, Mo and Sinopoli study an adversary who can read only a subset of control inputs and sensor outputs ([WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/detecting-integrity-attacks-on-control-systems-using-robust-physi/), abstract only). With one actuator and one networked sensor, that subset can be everything we have. The researchers infer that sensor B is what rescues our probes, since its echo of the watermark cannot be forged from the network. A field trial on WADI detected replay with a watermarked valve at a 100% true-positive rate, and its authors caution that the method does not suit bang-bang control ([Ahmed et al.](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/81324/1/Ahmed_etal_JPC_2022_A_practical_physical_watermarking_approach_to_detect_replay_attacks.pdf)). So our probes are on firmer ground in PID-valve mode than in on/off pump mode. Moving-target defences, which couple a secretly varying auxiliary subsystem to the plant ([Griffioen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.01423v2), abstract only), are interesting and too heavy for a hackathon.

### The pipeline game is real leak detection, and the weakest kind

The website's per-section mass balance is a simplified form of computational pipeline monitoring, which US rules require to follow API RP 1130 on hazardous-liquid lines ([49 CFR 195.444](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/49/195.444), secondary). Its field record is modest. In a US regulator study of 197 liquid pipeline incidents from 2010 to mid-2012, **monitoring software or SCADA was the first to identify the release in 23 cases, about 12%** ([Kiefner/PHMSA webinar](https://primis-meetings.phmsa.dot.gov/archive/Kiefner_PHMSA_Web_10-05-2012_(1)_PHMSA3.pdf), from webinar slides, since the final report could not be opened). Balance methods also cannot evaluate a loss when nothing flows through the meters ([API 1130, 2002 public copy](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/cfr/ibr/002/api.1130.2002.pdf), secondary). Real pipelines add pressure sensors that time the pressure drop travelling from a leak to both ends of a section. One vendor's comparison gives balance "section only" location and one to four hours of detection time, against roughly ±50 to 200 m and seconds for pressure-wave timing ([Rossma](https://pipeline.rossma.ru/en/blog/comparison-leak-detection)). Those are marketing figures from a single vendor, good for showing the direction of the trade-off and nothing more.

Two improvements follow for the game, both inference. The first costs almost nothing. A flow meter sits between two sections, so a meter that lies by some amount makes one neighbour look like it gains that amount and the other look like it loses it. A real leak shows a deficit in one section only. That "adjacent plus and minus pair" rule is derived from conservation, not from a cited source, and it brings the fault-or-attack question into the pipeline game, which today only asks where the leak is. An attacker who spoofs the two meters around one section by matched amounts can still fake a clean leak, and pressure sensors are what break that. The second improvement is a pressure-timing mini-game that locates the leak within a section. The location formula the researchers recorded is from memory and unverified, so check it against a textbook before it goes on the site.

## Buncefield already ran our scenario without an attacker

The closest real facility to our model is a fuel storage terminal, and the Buncefield investigation, read directly by the researcher, matches our layout almost part for part. Tank 912 had an automatic gauge displayed in the control room, which is our sensor A, and an independent high-level switch meant to close the inlet valves, which is our full switch. At 03:05 on 11 December 2005 the gauge "flatlined" while the tank kept filling, so none of its three alarms could fire. The independent switch was inoperable because a padlock that held its test lever in the working position had never been fitted. Petrol spilled at 05:37, and more than 250,000 litres escaped before the explosion ([HSE/COMAH Buncefield report](https://www.icheme.org/media/10706/buncefield-report.pdf)). The gauge had stuck 14 times in the preceding three and a half months. No attacker was involved.

This gives the project a headline scenario on its existing topology: a frozen gauge, a dormant switch and a steady metered inflow. The detector should flag zero variance during a known transfer, estimate the true level from the flows, show a countdown to overfill (Buncefield allowed about two and a half hours), and call it a fault because the signal is frozen and no network anomaly exists. Three cheap state variables come from the same report, all inference: a fault-history counter per instrument, since a repeat offender failing again is probably a fault; a "time since last proof test" on each switch, since one never seen to change state is unproven, not trusted; and a logged-or-unlogged flag on alarm-setpoint changes, since Buncefield's supervisors set some alarm levels themselves.

A real terminal differs from ours in three further ways. The site received fuel from three pipelines and controlled only one of them; shortly before the explosion one line's flow rose from 550 to 900 m³ per hour without the supervisors knowing ([HSE/COMAH Buncefield report](https://www.icheme.org/media/10706/buncefield-report.pdf)). A detector that treats every unexplained inflow change as an attack would be wrong at a real terminal, so an "external pipeline" mode is a useful false-alarm test. Second, terminals hold many tanks on shared headers. The researchers' strongest plant-level idea is that a stuck or passing valve moves product between tanks while the site-wide balance still closes, whereas a spoofed level on one tank breaks the site balance. Third, level data travels through a gauging network of tank bus, tank hub and control-room concentrator, with the overfill device wired separately to the shutdown system ([Emerson Rosemount article](https://s1-live.emerson.com/documents/automation/article-rosemount-tank-gauging-system-safety-efficiency-technical-article-en-81202.pdf)). That description is secondary and from one vendor. Its use to us is the inference that a compromised hub shifts several gauges at once, while a physical gauge fault affects one.

The safety layer is where our model is closest to practice and least explicit. An Emerson description of API 2350 says the most common tank category requires a separate, independent high-high alarm, and that an automated overfill prevention system stops inflow on high level with no manual action ([Emerson Automation Experts](https://www.emersonautomationexperts.com/2013/measurement-instrumentation/api-2350-overfill-prevention-systems/)). Our sensor B and full switch are that independent device in embryo; what is missing is a separate logic solver that trips the inlet by itself. TRITON shows the layer can be attacked too. Mandiant's write-up says the attacker reached a safety engineering workstation and reprogrammed Triconex controllers, and it recommends that such workstations never be dual-homed and that the controller key not be left in PROGRAM mode ([Mandiant](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/attackers-deploy-new-ics-attack-framework-triton)). A bypass or program-mode flag gives the detector a clean pair: a bypass left on after logged maintenance is a procedural fault, and a mode change with no work order is an attack indicator.

Two incidents add an outcome our detector cannot produce today. Colonial Pipeline's 2021 ransomware hit the IT network; CISA and the FBI found "no indications that the threat actor moved laterally to OT systems", yet the pipeline stopped for six days ([CISA AA21-131A](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa21-131a); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_Pipeline_ransomware_attack)). The 2022 Oiltanking incident limited operations at 13 German tank farms through IT systems, although sources disagree on how severe it was ([SecurityAffairs](https://securityaffairs.com/127493/hacking/oiltanking-gmbh-cyber-attack.html), secondary). A business-IT node would let the tool return "cyber incident in IT, process consistent with physics". For pipelines with remote stations, an RTU gathers the field devices and reports over a backhaul such as radio or satellite, and older RTUs are a weak point ([Yokogawa](https://www.yokogawa.com/be-nl/library/resources/media-publications/cyber-security-for-pipelines-other-scada-systems/)). The inference is a "stale versus fresh-but-wrong" discriminator: interference produces gaps and a comm-fail flag, while an attack delivers complete, timely data that contradicts local hard-wired sensors.

The Brunei context sharpens the pitch and corrects the earlier report, which could not confirm that energy is covered. The Cybersecurity Order, 2023 lists services relating to energy first among ten essential services. Section 16 obliges owners of designated critical information infrastructure to report prescribed incidents and to establish mechanisms to detect cybersecurity threats, with a fine of up to $100,000 for failing to notify ([Cybersecurity Order, 2023](https://aipalync.org/document/view/1813)). A verdict field that reads "maintenance work order" for a fault and "draft incident notification" for an attack speaks to that duty. Say "supports", because no public list shows which facilities are designated, and the reporting deadline sits in regulations that were not found. Nothing public was found on control systems at Brunei LNG or Hengyi, so the site should show a generic "Brunei fuel terminal" and not imply a real one.

| 7 | Zoned network view with two or three detectors and a flat-versus-zoned toggle | Purdue, IEC 62443, sensor placement | Medium | Entry-point localisation; the clearest visual for judges | Placement facts checked; fingerprint matrix is inference |
| 8 | Explicit safety layer with trip valve and bypass flag | SIS practice, TRITON, API 2350 | Medium | Makes sensor B and the switches a named protection layer; adds procedural-fault versus tampering | Mandiant checked; standards secondary |
| 9 | Second tank in series with its own controller | SWaT, DTS200, C-Town | Medium to high | Residual patterns in place of one residual; a spoof must stay consistent across tanks | Topologies checked or secondary; the discrimination claim is inference |
| 10 | External uncontrolled inflow; shared-manifold tank farm | Buncefield, tank terminals | Low, then medium | A false-alarm test; site-wide balance separating misrouting from spoofing | Hydraulics checked; discriminator is inference |
| 11 | Pressure-timing mini-game | Pipeline leak detection | Medium | Locates a leak within a section; defeats matched two-meter spoofing | Vendor data; formula unverified |
| 12 | Remote RTU over a lossy link | Pipeline SCADA | Medium | "Stale versus fresh-but-wrong" discriminator | Checked (Yokogawa) plus inference |
| Skip | Graph neural networks, moving-target defence, wireless link quality, edge detector at the device | Research literature | High | Little added for 4 to 8 sections and one tank | Abstracts, inference or unverified |

Ranks 1 to 6 share one property: none changes the plant model. They add scenarios, one estimator and a few state variables to what already exists, so they are safe to build late and easy to demonstrate. Rank 2 deserves priority among them, because it is the only item backed by a proven theorem and it fixes the single-sentence weakness of the current design, that two sensors can disagree but cannot outvote each other. It also pairs with the stateful CUSUM test that the project's earlier detection-methods research ranked first, since both run on the same anchored mass-balance model.

Ranks 7 and 9 are the two real topology changes, and they answer different questions. The zoned network answers "where did it come in?" and is mostly a website and bookkeeping change: hosts get a level, attacks get a path, and conduits get counters. It leaves the hard in-zone attacks exactly as hard as before, and the demo should say so. The second tank answers "what is it?" with more confidence, and it is the only upgrade that lets the detector reason without trusting a hard-wired sensor. It costs the most, because every existing check, scenario and evaluation run must be extended to two tanks. A team short on time should take rank 7 for the pitch and treat rank 9 as the stretch goal.

## Conclusion

The comparison changes how the current design should be described. It is not a toy awaiting a real topology; it is a faithful miniature of one cell of a real terminal, with the gauge, the independent switch and the hard-wired backup that Buncefield and overfill-protection practice describe. What real plants add around that cell is context: a neighbour whose physics must agree, a boundary that records who crossed it, and a safety layer with its own state. Each of those gives the detector a new kind of witness, and the fault-or-attack question is at bottom a question of how many independent witnesses agree.

The research also marks the edge of what topology can buy. More tanks, more zones and more sensors raise the number of things an attacker must falsify consistently, which is what the 40-of-43 replay result measures. None of them resolves the case of a careful spoof on one channel with a clean network, and none of the notes found a source that tests fault-versus-attack discrimination on a coupled plant. That gap is an opening. A two-tank simulator that reports which faults and attacks its residual pattern separates, and which it cannot, would be a small original result, and a more honest centrepiece than a single accuracy figure.

## Addendum: reporting an attack in Brunei

*Added 11 November 2026 at the project owner's request. Contact details were read from the official BruCERT and Cyber Security Brunei websites on that date; the legal duty was read from the gazette text of the Cybersecurity Order, 2023; the roles of the safety regulators come from search summaries only.*

The detector's verdict decides who gets the call, and in Brunei the attack branch has a named destination. This turns the topology question into a workflow the project can show end to end: detect, make safe, report.

**When the verdict is ATTACK**

1. **Make the plant safe.** Switch the pump or valve to manual and cut the control network off from outside connections, since the attacker usually arrives over the network. Keep the logs and the detector's reasons as evidence.
2. **Tell the company's own incident team.** The duty to report sits with the owner of the system, not with the operator on shift.
3. **Report to BruCERT**, the national computer emergency response team inside Cyber Security Brunei (CSB). Checked on brucert.org.bn: hotline (+673) 245 8001, second line (+673) 717 0766, email cert@brucert.org.bn, and an online incident form at [brucert.org.bn/form/incident-reporting](https://www.brucert.org.bn/form/incident-reporting). The form asks for the incident date and whether the matter has been reported to law enforcement.
4. **Critical sites must notify the Commissioner of Cybersecurity.** The Cybersecurity Order, 2023 lists "services relating to energy" first among ten essential services. Under section 16, the owner of a system designated as critical information infrastructure (CII) must notify the Commissioner of prescribed incidents affecting it, or any interconnected system under the owner's control, within a prescribed period, and must have mechanisms to detect threats and incidents. Failing to notify carries a fine of up to B$100,000, up to 2 years' imprisonment, or both. CSB's Code of Practice page now refers to the law as the Cybersecurity Act, Chapter 272 ([csb.gov.bn/code-practice](https://www.csb.gov.bn/code-practice)), and CSB reported releasing version 2.0 of its Code of Practice for CII in September 2026.
5. **If a crime was committed, report it to the Royal Brunei Police Force as well.**

**When the verdict is FAULT**

No cyber report is needed: raise a maintenance work order. If the fault caused a safety incident, it goes to the safety regulators instead. Search summaries indicate that SHENA (the Safety, Health and Environment National Authority) covers workplace safety and incident reporting, and that the Petroleum Authority of Brunei Darussalam regulates upstream, midstream and downstream oil and gas operations and receives operators' HSE reports. Their reporting deadlines and forms were not found.

**What is still not confirmed**

- The exact reporting deadline ("prescribed period") and which incidents are "prescribed". These are set in subsidiary rules that were not found.
- Whether any particular Brunei facility is designated CII. Designations are not public, so the project should say it "supports" the reporting decision, not that a named plant is covered.
- Whether the second BruCERT number is a WhatsApp line. A 2023 press notice says so; the official site lists the number without a label.

**What this adds to the project**

The detector's advice for every ATTACK verdict now names BruCERT and the Commissioner. The website has a "In Brunei, who do you tell?" section on the Story tab, a line in the Level 2 answer pop-up, and a practice "Draft report for BruCERT" filled in from the run when you watch an attack. This is the report's upgrade number 6 ("report to CSB?" output), built.

## Addendum: risk review of every box, and a stress test against an adaptive attacker (23 November 2026)

*Every box on the map was questioned against classic failures and four current threats: AI agents, AI-generated data, AI-assisted reverse engineering, and swarms (many drones, or many sites at once). Threat facts come from 2026 reporting (Booz Allen's OT lab test, Dragos, the Forescout WAGO case, CISA advisories, refinery drone strikes) and are mostly secondary sources. The stress-test numbers are measured with this project's simulator.*

### What changed on the map
- **Two missing threat boxes**: *Supplier / vendor* and *Cellular modem / remote access*, with an attack path from the modem around the data diode into the field network. Both are real today: the French blood bank attack (November 2026) came through a refrigeration supplier's component, and Dragos and CISA flagged cellular gateways as a 2026 blind spot.
- **A physical-attack path**: *Drone / sabotage* to Tank 1. Drone swarms hit refineries repeatedly in 2026; today the detector would call physical damage a FAULT.
- **Known-weakness markers (red !)** on the virtual level, the 2-out-of-3 vote and the detector, with the reason in each box's pop-up on the website.

### The weaknesses found, most serious first
1. **The virtual level is not independent.** It is worked out from the pump feedback and the flow meter, which travel over the network, so a coordinated forgery fools it and the 2-out-of-3 vote can be two liars against one honest sensor. Fix: work it out from hard-wired signals (motor current, the power monitor).
2. **The detector's code is public**, so an attacker or AI agent can rehearse against it offline. The secret probe schedule can also be learned by reverse-engineering the controller. Fix: site-secret settings, and let the detector choose probe times with a site secret.
3. **Learned thresholds can be poisoned** if the attacker is present while they are learned. Fix: learn only from checked periods, sign the result.
4. **No "physical damage" answer**: drone or sabotage damage is called a FAULT.
5. **An attack disguised as a power cut** (a remotely tripped breaker) is called a FAULT, because the outage is real.
6. **"First rule wins"** can mislabel overlapping events (a fault and an attack together, or a swarm across tanks).
7. **Nothing watches the detector**: if it stops, nobody is told. Fix: a heartbeat alarm.
8. Approved settings (setpoints, API 2350 levels) can be changed; sensors A and B may share a vendor and power supply; logs can be wiped first; a "diode" may be a software firewall or be bypassed; the radio link can be jammed; forced repeated safety trips stop production while looking safe; operators may over-trust the detector or be flooded with alerts; attackers may pose as BruCERT.
9. **The headline accuracy is measured against attackers who do not adapt.**

### Stress test: "AI consistent forgery"
A new stress scenario (`forge`, run with `python "FAULT OR ATTACK.py" --stress [--realistic]`) models an attacker on an already-trusted device who forces the pump on and runs a "digital twin" of a healthy tank: sensor A, the motor feedback and the valve position are forged so that every networked signal agrees with the physics the detector checks, including the secret probes' dips, which the attacker sees on the network. No new device appears and no foreign writes are sent. It is scored apart from the headline accuracy so earlier results stay comparable. Full output: `reports/stress_consistent_forgery.txt` (20 seeds x 3 duration modes = 60 runs per row).

A new **hard-wired witness check** (`witness_check`, on by default since 23 November 2026) trusts only what the network cannot reach: if sensor A's mass balance closes neatly while backup sensor B gains water the forged feedback cannot explain, or the hard-wired high-level switch says "full" while sensor A reads normal, the networked story is a lie.

| Plant | Witness check | Backup sensor B | Called ATTACK | Caught before overflow |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Simple | off | off | 5/60 | 2/60 |
| Simple | off | on | 2/60 | 2/60 |
| Simple | on | off | 15/60 | 2/60 |
| Simple | **on** | **on** | **60/60** | **60/60** (mean 7 s) |
| Realistic | off | off | 13/60 | 12/60 |
| Realistic | off | on | 7/60 | 7/60 |
| Realistic | on | off | 32/60 | 12/60 |
| Realistic | **on** | **on** | **60/60** | **60/60** (mean 14 s) |

What this shows:
- **Without the new check, the detector loses to this attacker.** Most runs end as UNKNOWN or a wrong FAULT label, and the tank overflows first.
- **With the check and a hard-wired backup sensor, every run is caught before the overflow.** Without sensor B, the check can only use the high-level switch, which trips when the tank is already full, so it names the attack but too late to stop the spill. Backup sensor B is not optional against this attacker.
- **The check does not break anything else.** Over the full evaluation (20 seeds, both plants) it raised no false alarms (0 in 10 healthy hours per plant) and changed no correct call to a wrong one. It improved the overall score from 1433/1440 to 1439/1440 on the simple plant and from 1303/1440 to 1341/1440 on the realistic plant, mostly because the replay-with-fresh-noise attack is now caught more often (realistic plant: 64/120 to 101/120). It was then switched on by default, and the README, the saved evaluations, the dashboard and the website were refreshed with the new numbers.
- **Limit**: an attacker who can also reach backup sensor B (physical access, an insider, or a shared vendor weakness) defeats this check. Independence of sensor B is the whole defence.

### The physical side (23 November 2026)
The detector was cyber-focused. Three physical gaps from the risk review are now built in the simulator and scored separately (`--physical`, results in `reports/physical_side.txt`):
- **On-site tampering.** Tamper switches (on instrument cabinets and sensor B's housing) and the bypass flag, with no work order, give **PHYSICAL**. This closes the hole where an insider rewires sensor B to defeat the hard-wired witness check.
- **Site-access fusion.** Gate, door and badge events are fused like network evidence: a symptom soon after unplanned access, with nothing odd on the network, is an on-site cause (a valve forced by hand, a theft tap). Planned work under a work order stays a FAULT.
- **Physical damage.** Several independent hard-wired signs within five seconds (sensor B dead, gas alarm, bund filling fast) mean damage such as a drone strike, not wear.
- **Slow faults.** A test showed leaks under about 0.5% of the tank per second (a quarter of normal flow) were never detected, because the per-second checks ignore errors that small. A five-minute slow-balance check, where sensor noise cancels out, now catches a corrosion leak starting at 0.02%/s in about three minutes, separates it from theft (bund filling versus bund dry after unplanned access), and catches water ingress with the water-interface probe.

All six misses are one older false call (the per-second roughness rule, seed 6). A wider check of 25 healthy hours per plant found one such false call on the simple plant and two (replay rule) on the realistic plant; none came from the new physical or slow checks. Drone and perimeter radar is drawn on the map as partner technology, not built.

**Addition (same day): corrosion before it leaks, and the standards behind the physical side.** An electrical-resistance corrosion probe is now a hard-wired input. ### How regulators elsewhere draw the same line (23 November 2026)
Two regulator documents support the fault / attack / physical split this project is built on:
- **UK, HSE L30**, *A guide to the Offshore Installations (Safety Case) Regulations 2005* (3rd edition, 2006). It defines safety-critical elements as parts of an installation and its plant "(including computer programmes)" whose failure could cause or contribute substantially to a major accident, or which exist to prevent or limit one. These are subject to independent and competent verification throughout the installation's life, and the safety case gets a thorough review at least every five years. So control and safety software is a major-accident matter, not only an IT one. Note: the 2005 Regulations were largely replaced by the 2015 Offshore Safety Directive version (guidance L154); check which applies before citing L30 as current law.
- **Norway, Havtil**, *Safety and Responsibility: Understanding the Norwegian Regime* (2024). The regulator (the Petroleum Safety Authority until 2024) separates safety ("preventing accidents and mishaps") from security ("countering undesirable intentional incidents/deliberate attacks"). It supervises both: Petroleum Act section 9-3 requires security measures and emergency plans against deliberate attacks, and since 2023 Havtil enforces the Security Act for petroleum. It also requires barriers to be "monitored, measured and followed up" so they stay fully functional, and it receives 600–800 incident notifications a year on a 24-hour line.

What this means for the design: the detector's FAULT (accident), ATTACK and PHYSICAL (deliberate, by network or on site) answers follow the split these regulators already use, and its heartbeat, bypass flag and hard-wired safety layer are barriers being monitored. The detector itself stays advisory, so it is unlikely to count as a safety-critical element, but it watches the ones that are.
