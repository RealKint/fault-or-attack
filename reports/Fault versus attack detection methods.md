# Make the cyber layer explain the physics

Researchers, vendors and standards bodies agree on one idea. **A physical fault leaves the cyber layer consistent while the physical layer breaks. An attack makes the cyber layer disagree with physics, with the controller's own logic, or with an independent channel.** Academic work puts this into practice with stateful residual tests (CUSUM), paired controller-side and plant-side detectors, active "watermark" probing and cyber-plus-physical evidence fusion. Commercial OT monitors (Dragos, Nozomi, Microsoft Defender for IoT) detect anomalies well but never output a FAULT/ATTACK label. Their alerts often say an event "might be an operational issue or an attempt to manipulate the device", and a human makes the call. That gap is the niche "Fault or Attack" fills. The prototype's rules already cover a hand-built version of most passive techniques. Its two known holes, the drift-shaped spoof and noisy replay, are the cases the literature says passive, stateless checks cannot close. The highest-value additions are, in order: a two-sided CUSUM on an open-loop "anchored" mass balance; a plant-side check that the pump command matches the on/off law; an actuator-consistency correlation and noise-fingerprint test against noisy replay; a randomized-threshold watermark that turns replay into an obvious mismatch; and a naive-Bayes evidence combiner with an UNCERTAIN verdict. One case cannot be solved passively: an attacker who adds a smooth ramp to live readings looks exactly like drift. The tool should say so honestly and fall back on cyber evidence. The current 99% figure comes from the prototype's own scenarios. It will not survive an adaptive attacker unless the evaluation adds adversarial scenarios and Urbina-style impact metrics.

## Researchers separate faults from attacks by asking which layer breaks

Most published work is plain anomaly detection. Classifying the *cause* is rarer, and it clusters into a few families. The base family is the **model-based residual with a stateful test**. Urbina et al. (ACM CCS 2016) modelled a SWaT water tank from first principles as h_{k+1} = h_k + (Q_in − Q_out)/Area. They then attacked it with an adversary who raised the reported level more slowly than the real level, so the PLC's high cutoff fired late and the tank overflowed. A stateless test (alarm if |r| > τ) let the tank spill unless its threshold was so tight that false alarms became frequent. A **nonparametric CUSUM, S_{k+1} = max(0, S_k + |r_k| − δ) with δ = 0.002, prevented spilling at an expected time between false alarms of about 170 minutes**. Only the stateful test caught a pump that ignored OFF commands while reporting OFF ([Urbina et al. 2016](https://users.soe.ucsc.edu/%7Ealacarde/papers/ccs16.pdf)). Urbina does not label faults versus attacks. Instead, the CUSUM forces a stealthy attacker to stay within δ of the model every step, which caps the damage per unit time. Their survey also found that **"the vast majority of previous work uses stateless tests"**, which is the regime the prototype's per-step mass-balance roughness check is in now.

The second family discriminates directly. Xue, Shen, Ding and Zhao (arXiv 2025) pair a normal controller-side fault detector with a **plant-side detector that recomputes what the controller should have commanded from the measured output and compares it with the command the actuator actually received**. A fault produces a corrupted measurement, but the control input stays consistent with the controller law, so only the controller-side detector fires. Stealthy replay, covert and zero-dynamics attacks are built to fit the plant, not the controller, so only the plant-side detector fires: "faults are only detected by the controller-side detector" ([Xue et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.14052)). The authors admit that a replay and a fault happening together is ambiguous, and they recommend adding watermarking. Taheri et al. (IEEE TCST 2024) use the same two-sided structure with unknown-input observers and stress "an inherent difference between machine induced faults and cyber attacks" ([arXiv 2009.06196](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2009.06196)).

The third family is **active probing**. Mo and Sinopoli (2009) showed that in a stable closed loop, replayed data is statistically indistinguishable from live data, so they inject a secret random signal into the actuator and check that sensors echo it ([arXiv 2103.00790](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.00790); [Liu et al. CDC 2018](https://people.kth.se/~kallej/papers/secure_cdc18liu.pdf)). Satchidanandan and Kumar push this further. Without private excitation, a malicious sensor that knows the control law can report a self-consistent trajectory with correctly distributed noise, and **no passive detector can catch it** ([arXiv 1606.08741](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.08741)). Zhang, Kasis, Polycarpou and Parisini (IFAC SAFEPROCESS 2022) use a switching watermark with a two-layer threshold. Any threat crosses the first threshold, and the watermark seeds are designed so that only replay crosses the second. This separates **sensor replay from sensor bias faults**, which ordinary detectors confuse ([Zhang et al. 2022](https://zenodo.org/records/7263349)). The one field deployment, on the WADI testbed, used a two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test on residuals and reached **100% true-positive rate with 250-sample windows**. It also warns that watermarking "can not be applied to all types of control, especially in case of a bang-bang control" ([Ahmed et al. 2022](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/81324/1/Ahmed_etal_JPC_2022_A_practical_physical_watermarking_approach_to_detect_replay_attacks.pdf)). That warning applies to the prototype's 40/60 hysteresis controller, so the watermark has to be redesigned around switching events (below).

The remaining families combine evidence. Ahsan et al. (arXiv 2026) separate DER faults from attacks by cross-matching code dependency graphs, network captures, logs and field measurements. **Logs consistent with the field deviation mean a fault. Network data inconsistent with the field means false-data injection** ([Ahsan et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.03289)). Mosaffa and Khaloozadeh declare an attack only when a physical fault-detection alarm coincides with abnormal latency, loss or traffic ([magiran](https://magiran.com/paper/2466014/?lang=en)). Chockalingam et al. built an expert-validated **Bayesian network for floodgate operators that outputs "intentional attack vs accidental technical failure"**. It combines technical evidence with context such as maintenance state and physical access control. The model is published as `intentionalattacks` in the R package bnRep ([TU Delft](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:5926ebb8-9f98-4fd9-98cc-e670990def83); [bnRep](https://rdrr.io/cran/bnRep/man/intentionalattacks.html)). Provan and Orizio encode each fault and each attack as a separate mode of a switched linear model and diagnose by finding which mode best explains the data ([CENICS 2017](https://personales.upv.es/thinkmind/CENICS/CENICS_2017/cenics_2017_4_40_68006.html)). Heavy machine learning shows up mostly in power-grid work and gives modest results. On the ORNL/MSU disturbance-versus-attack data, a Random Forest reached **90.56%** ([arXiv 2307.03323](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.03323)). A Boise State study on TEP found heavy overlap between fault and attack states in PCA space ([Boise State](https://experts.boisestate.edu/en/publications/fault-tolerance-vs-attack-detection-in-industrial-control-systems/)).

## Vendors detect anomalies but leave the verdict to humans

Commercial OT monitoring rests on passive capture, protocol deep packet inspection and several engines layered together. Dragos splits detection into Configuration, Modeling, Indicator and Threat Behavior types ([Dragos whitepaper](https://www.manufacturing.net/home/whitepaper/13245081/dragos-the-four-types-of-threat-detection)). It treats adversary-behaviour analytics as primary and warns that anomaly detection alone produces "a high volume of false positives" (vendor claim). It also concedes that modeling detections surface "misconfigurations or failing assets" as well as attacks ([Dragos blog](https://www.dragos.com/blog/nerc-cip-015-compliance-detect-anomalous-activity-with-dragos-platform)). Nozomi Guardian runs separate network and process learning goals. Each switches from a learning phase to a protection phase, and the system raises `VI:PROC:NEW-VALUE` when a process variable takes a value never seen before ([Nozomi docs](https://technicaldocs.nozominetworks.com/products/n2os/topics/administration/settings/scp/c_n2os_admin_settings_scp_edit_learning.html); [alert reference](https://technicaldocs.nozominetworks.com/products/n2os/topics/autogenerated/security_features/r_n2os_alerts_virtual-image.html)).

Microsoft Defender for IoT is the clearest public example of splitting operational events from security events. Its five engines are Policy violation, Protocol violation, **Operational** ("intermittent connectivity that can indicate early signs of equipment failure"), Malware and Anomaly. Each alert carries a severity, a category, an ATT&CK for ICS technique and an explicit rate threshold. Examples: **8 failed attempts in 5 minutes** marks a device as unresponsive (T0881), **10 restarts per hour** trips "Excessive Restart Rate", and **20 exceptions per hour** trips "Abnormal Exception Pattern". Several alert texts carry the dual wording "might be the result of an operational issue or an attempt to manipulate the device" ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/defender-for-iot/alert-engine-messages)). The closest thing to a government fault-versus-attack classifier is the DOE/INL CyOTE program. It moves from observable to anomaly to "triggering event" to decision, and its OPTIC process helps staff decide whether an anomaly is "possible malicious activity or merely a maintenance related irregularity" ([DOE CyOTE](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2021-09/CyOTE_Fact_Sheet_508.pdf); [INL](https://inlsoftware.inl.gov/product/cyote)). None of the notes found a vendor that publicly documents an automatic binary fault/attack classifier. That absence is observed, not proven.

MITRE's ATT&CK for ICS supports the physics-first approach. For **T0832 Manipulation of View, MITRE states that no standard detection method currently exists** ([MITRE DET0785](https://attack.mitre.org/detectionstrategies/DET0785)). For T0856 Spoof Reporting Message, it recommends checking protocol content "against expected values or out-of-band process data" ([MITRE T0856](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T0856)). That is a mass-balance cross-check described in security language. A third-party mirror reports that T0856 has been superseded by T1692.002, so check current IDs on attack.mitre.org before putting them on a slide ([Glexia](https://security.glexia.com/learn/attack/techniques/T1692.002-reporting-message)). On the standards side, the SANS ICS Five Critical Controls list ICS-specific incident response first and protocol-aware network visibility third ([Dragos summary](https://www.dragos.com/insights/five-critical-controls)). IEC 62443-3-3 requires continuous monitoring (SR 6.2) and deterministic output to a predefined safe state (SR 3.6) ([UpGuard](https://www.upguard.com/blog/isa-62443-3-3-2013); [ISA TOC](https://www.isa.org/getmedia/4379f3a1-2687-4a0a-9d0f-20d743233c1b/ISA-62443-3-3_TOC.pdf)). CISA tells operators to plan to run or shut down safely because "the ICS may be working against the process" ([CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/ics-alerts/ir-alert-h-16-056-01)).

Real incidents show both kinds of misclassification. At Petro Rabigh, a June 2017 Triconex trip was blamed on a mechanical failure, and the Triton attackers stayed in place until a second trip in August ([Dark Reading](https://www.darkreading.com/cyberattacks-data-breaches/triton-trisis-attack-was-more-widespread-than-publicly-known)). Stuxnet recorded about 21 seconds of normal sensor values and replayed them in a loop, so operators and alarms saw normal readings ([Foreign Policy / Langner](https://foreignpolicy.com/2013/11/19/stuxnets-secret-twin/)). Colonial Pipeline shut down OT "out of an abundance of caution" after an IT-only ransomware incident ([CSO Online](https://www.csoonline.com/article/3618016/colonial-pipeline-shutdown-highlights-need-for-better-ot-cybersecurity-practices.html)). The Triton case argues for never closing an unexplained trip as FAULT without checking for concurrent engineering-network activity. Colonial shows that over-calling ATTACK also costs money, which makes a strong reliability pitch for the Hibiscus "Safety and Operational Reliability" framing. For Brunei, the **Cybersecurity Order, 2023 lets the Minister designate Critical Information Infrastructure and imposes incident-reporting, audit and code-of-practice duties** on CII owners ([Order text](https://aipalync.org/document/view/1813)). Fast, evidence-backed triage supports that duty directly. The notes did not confirm whether oil and gas appears in the Order's Schedule or what the reporting deadline is.

## A perfect drift-mimicking spoof is beyond passive detection

The literature names the prototype's known weakness as a fundamental limit. If an attacker controls every sensor and actuator, residuals can stay at zero while the plant is driven anywhere ([Urbina et al. 2016](https://users.soe.ucsc.edu/%7Ealacarde/papers/ccs16.pdf)). Without private excitation, a malicious sensor can fabricate a consistent trajectory ([Satchidanandan & Kumar](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.08741)). A 2026 *Sensors* paper reports **chance-level discrimination for exactly matched sensor-fault/attack pairs** and argues for identifiability-aware triage instead of forced labels ([Sensors 26(17):5585](https://doi.org/10.3390/s26175585); search-snippet level only). Ahsan et al. also show that an attacker can alter packets to trigger the "fault" pattern on purpose ([arXiv 2601.03289](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.03289)). The notes found no published passive test that separates a slow-ramp false-data injection from genuine linear sensor drift.

The attack types need to be kept apart, because the remedies differ. **A replay or a fabricated stream is decoupled from the current plant.** It fails tests that compare the reported level with current actuation, with current outflow, or with a secret watermark, and adding fresh noise does not help the attacker. **An additive spoof, real reading plus a slow ramp, keeps every physical echo of the live plant**, including the watermark response, because the attacker modifies real data in transit. Against an honest drift fault in A, that spoof is physically identical by construction. What can still separate them is evidence outside the sensor value: a write path on the network, an authentication failure, a ramp whose rate or shape breaks drift physics (it changes at pump switches, reverses, or exceeds a plausible drift rate), or a context prior such as recent maintenance. When none of that is present, the correct output is **UNCERTAIN, escalate**, the CyOTE "triggering event" state, not a confident FAULT. Showing judges this distinction, and the scenario where the tool honestly declines to decide, is more credible than claiming 99% against every attacker.

### Ranks 1–2: stop letting the spoof re-anchor, and recompute the controller

The current mass-balance check compares each step's measured level change with the expected change. That is the stateless regime Urbina showed lets an attacker add up to τ per step without detection. The fix has two parts. First, replace the "linear running total" with a **signed two-sided CUSUM** on the one-step residual r_k = ΔL_A,k − (q_pump(feedback) − q_out(meter))·dt/A: S⁺_k = max(0, S⁺_{k−1} + r_k − δ) and S⁻_k = max(0, S⁻_{k−1} − r_k − δ). Alarm when either exceeds τ, then reset. Urbina used |r| ([Urbina et al. 2016](https://users.soe.ucsc.edu/%7Ealacarde/papers/ccs16.pdf)). The signed form targets a persistent one-direction bias, which is what a drift spoof is. Set δ to roughly half the smallest bias rate you want to catch, a Page-rule heuristic rather than something from the sources. Calibrate τ from long healthy runs so that the expected time between false alarms is at least ten times the demo length. Second, add an **open-loop anchored model**: L_model(t) = L(t₀) + Σ(q_in − q_out)·dt/A, integrated from a trusted anchor and re-zeroed only at independent events such as agreement with sensor B or a known start. Track D(t) = L_A(t) − L_model(t). Meter noise makes integrator error grow like σ√t, while a ramp spoof grows like b·t, so CUSUM on D(t)/√t, or on D directly, exposes cumulative bias that the per-step residual hides. Urbina found that **physical LDS models were about an order of magnitude better than AR models** at limiting stealthy impact, and models that correlate several signals did better still, so feed the pump power monitor into the inflow estimate too (pumped volume ∝ energy at constant head).

```python
class Cusum2:
    def __init__(s, delta, tau): s.d, s.t, s.p, s.n = delta, tau, 0.0, 0.0
    def step(s, r):
        s.p = max(0.0, s.p + r - s.d); s.n = max(0.0, s.n - r - s.d)
        hit = 'high' if s.p > s.t else 'low' if s.n > s.t else None
        if hit: s.p = s.n = 0.0
        return hit
```

The **plant-side command-law check** from Xue et al. costs almost nothing because the controller is a known rule. At the pump, recompute the expected command from the best available *true* level estimate (sensor B, or L_model when B is missing) and the previous pump state: expected = ON if est < 40, OFF if est > 60, else the previous state. Count the steps where the command the pump received differs from that, allowing a margin of a couple of percent around the thresholds for noise. The verdict follows Xue's logic ([Xue et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.14052)). If the command is consistent with the *reported* level but the reported level disagrees with physics, the controller was fooled by a bad sensor: that is FAULT evidence unless other checks say spoof. If the command is inconsistent with the controller law applied to *any* level reading, something between the controller and the pump changed it: that is ATTACK. This generalizes the existing "pump running without command" and "commanded but not running" rules into a single residual.

### Ranks 3–4: beat noisy replay without changing the plant

Replayed data is decoupled from current actuation, and a natural variation in outflow demand makes the decoupling visible. Over a sliding window of N = 60 samples, compute the Pearson correlation between dL_A/dt and the commanded net inflow (q_pump(feedback) − q_out(meter))/A. A live sensor, healthy or drifting, gives **ρ close to 1**, because drift adds a slowly varying offset that differencing mostly removes. A replay gives ρ near zero or negative, however much fresh noise the attacker adds. A frozen or stuck sensor gives an undefined ρ (zero variance), which the existing flatline rule already handles. The test is weak when outflow is constant and the pump is in a long steady phase, so require at least one pump transition inside the window before the result counts. The countermeasure notes propose this test; it applies the watermarking literature's rationale that replay has the right statistics but the wrong coupling ([Liu et al. CDC 2018](https://people.kth.se/~kallej/papers/secure_cdc18liu.pdf)). The notes found no paper that benchmarks it on a hysteresis loop, so validate it in the harness.

The **noise fingerprint** catches attackers who get the noise wrong. With sensor noise of ±0.3% (if uniform, σ ≈ 0.3/√3 ≈ 0.17%; check the simulator's noise model), take the high-pass residual (first difference minus its local mean) over N = 60 and apply a two-sided χ² test on (N−1)s²/σ². A synthetic smooth spoof reads too low. A replay with fresh noise added on top of the old noise reads about 2σ² unless the attacker denoises first. Add a sign test inspired by Bonczek and Bezzo's CUSIGN detector, which notes that stealthy attacks under a χ² threshold still leave non-random residual sign patterns ([arXiv 2005.07821](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.07821)): count positive mass-balance residuals n⁺ over N = 60 and alarm if |n⁺ − 30| > 3.3·√60/2 ≈ 12.8. The exact CUSIGN statistic was not retrieved, so this is a standard-statistics stand-in. Lag-1 autocorrelation of the high-pass residual completes the set, because real white sensor noise sits near zero there.

### Rank 5: features that make a spoof look less like drift

These features do not resolve the additive-ramp case, but they give the fusion layer evidence. **Drift physics:** a real drift is monotonic, slow, indifferent to the control cycle, and continuous across pump ON/OFF transitions. Estimate the A–B divergence slope separately over each pump phase. Add ATTACK evidence if the slope changes at switch events, reverses sign, starts or stops abruptly, or exceeds a stated plausible bound. The notes found no published drift-rate bound for level transmitters, so pick one, for example 0.05%/min, and label it as an assumption. **Goal-directedness:** Urbina's attacker shapes the bias so the high cutoff fires late ([Urbina et al. 2016](https://users.soe.ucsc.edu/%7Ealacarde/papers/ccs16.pdf)). Measure the fraction of time the reported level sits within ±1% of 40% or 60% compared with the healthy baseline, and the projected true excursion (from B or L_model) beyond the safe band while the deviation persists. A reading that hovers just below 60% while L_model passes 65% is ATTACK-flavoured. Note one caveat: an honest drift in A also pushes the real level out of the band, so the excursion alone is not discriminating. Hovering and timing are what separate the two. The notes found no published metric for goal-directedness; this is our own design. **Coordinated-agreement rule:** if A and B agree with each other but both disagree with the mass balance and power monitor, two independent sensors failing identically is improbable, so treat it as ATTACK evidence. A patent states the converse principle: a real component fault shows on several sensors, while a fault at only one suggests spoofing ([US 10372569](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10372569)).

### Rank 6: a watermark the on/off controller can carry

The textbook Gaussian watermark needs a continuous actuator, and the WADI authors rule out bang-bang control ([Ahmed et al. 2022](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/81324/1/Ahmed_etal_JPC_2022_A_practical_physical_watermarking_approach_to_detect_replay_attacks.pdf)). Trapiello et al.'s pseudo-random binary watermark suggests a binary version is feasible ([IRI-UPC](https://www.iri.upc.edu/download/scidoc/2217), snippet only). No published watermark for a hysteresis level controller was found, so the following designs are untested proposals. **Randomized thresholds (cheapest):** at each switch, draw the next ON threshold θ_on,j = 40 + U(−2, 2) and the next OFF threshold θ_off,j = 60 + U(−2, 2) from a secret seed held inside the controller. Under an honest sensor, the reported level should turn around at the current secret threshold, give or take noise and pump lag, so its local peak after an OFF command (or trough after an ON command) lands near θ_j. Testing "the reported level at the commanded switch time" would be useless, because the controller switches *when* the reported value crosses θ_j, so that value matches even under replay. Test the turning point instead. Under replay, the reported trace keeps rising or falling after the command and turns at the *old* thresholds, so e_j = (reported turning level − θ_j) is about U(−2, 2) plus noise rather than about 0.17%. Alarm when Σe_j²/σ_e² over the last m = 4 cycles exceeds the χ²₄ 99.9% quantile, about 18.5, with σ_e calibrated from healthy runs including pump-lag overshoot. The cost is about ±2% extra swing and no extra switching. **Test pulses (stronger, costs wear):** while the pump is OFF and the level is between 45% and 55%, fire secret Bernoulli(0.1–0.2) pulses of 1–2 s. Compute the matched-filter gain S = Σe_k·ΔL_k / Σe_k², which should be about b, the known per-pulse rise, for a live sensor and about 0 for replay or fabrication. This is the simplified form of Satchidanandan and Kumar's two variance tests ([arXiv 1606.08741](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.08741)). Both designs fail if the attacker owns the controller and learns the seed, and neither catches the additive ramp on live data, because that data still carries the echo. A **drifting-but-honest sensor echoes the watermark with an offset; a replayed or fabricated stream does not echo it at all**. That pair is the cleanest fault/attack separator for the replay family, matching the Zhang et al. result ([Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/7263349)).

### Rank 7: replace hard labels with P(attack)

Industry practice and the BN literature both say that a single anomaly is ambiguous and that corroboration produces confidence. Build a naive-Bayes combiner in pure Python. Each existing and new check becomes a boolean or binned feature with hand-set likelihoods P(feature | FAULT) and P(feature | ATTACK), expert-elicited as in the Chockalingam network ([TU Delft](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:5926ebb8-9f98-4fd9-98cc-e670990def83)). Sum log-likelihood ratios onto a prior and output P(attack). Add context nodes the BN uses: maintenance due, sensor age, recent firmware change, open remote session. Microsoft's "Firmware Version Changed" alert warns that such a change "might be authorized activity, for example a planned maintenance procedure" ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/defender-for-iot/alert-engine-messages)). Add a **temporal-coupling feature** following Mosaffa and Ahsan: the lag in steps between the first network anomaly (new source, write burst, packet-rate change, write to a coil or setpoint register) and the first physical anomaly. A physical anomaly within ±k steps of a network event raises the attack probability. A physical anomaly on a quiet network is weak FAULT evidence, because a compromised endpoint produces no network trace. Map the output to three bands: ATTACK above about 0.8, FAULT below about 0.2, and **UNCERTAIN, escalate** in between. That third band reproduces the CyOTE triggering-event step and is where the additive ramp should land. Show the top three contributing features as the "reason" line in the UI.

### Ranks 8–9: cryptographic freshness and the language OT people speak

Make each sensor frame (sensor_id, seq, t_send, value, tag), with tag = HMAC-SHA256(key, id‖seq‖t‖value) truncated to 8–16 bytes, using Python's `hmac` and `hashlib`. Reject a frame on a bad tag, on seq ≤ last_seq, or on clock skew greater than two sample periods. This follows DNP3 Secure Authentication v5, which uses sequence numbers and fresh challenge data against replay ([DNP3 SAv5](https://icscsi.org/library/Documents/ICS_Protocols/DNP3%20Secure%20Authentication%20v5%202011-11-08.pdf)). An authentication failure is an ATTACK signature that faults essentially never produce. The demo should also state the limit: a compromised sensor or PLC holds the key and signs lies, which is why the physics checks stay. Urbina observed that **95% of traffic at a real US water facility was unauthenticated Modbus** ([Urbina et al. 2016](https://users.soe.ucsc.edu/~alacarde/papers/ccs16.pdf)), so make the MAC a toggle and show the detector working without it.

For credibility, tag every verdict with an engine category (operational, protocol, policy, anomaly) and an ATT&CK for ICS ID: false level as T0856, HMI-normal-while-overflowing as T0832, unknown-host pump write as T0855, setpoint change as T0836, and flood with unresponsive controller as T0814/T0813 ([MITRE T0856](https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T0856); [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/defender-for-iot/alert-engine-messages)). Use Microsoft-style "X events in Y minutes" rate thresholds for the network checks, and a Nozomi-style learning phase that baselines ranges, rates and talkers during the first N ticks before switching to protection. Attach a playbook. FAULT: raise a work order and switch to the redundant sensor. ATTACK: go to a safe state with local control (IEC 62443 SR 3.6; CISA), stop trusting network values, isolate the source, preserve evidence, and generate an incident-report stub (time, asset, technique, evidence) to support the Brunei Order's reporting duty ([Order text](https://aipalync.org/document/view/1813)). UNCERTAIN: joint review by operations and security.

### Rank 11: the principled long-term detector

A multiple-model bank runs one one-step predictor per hypothesis: nominal, each fault mode (leak: extra −q_leak; stuck: constant y; drift: y = h + c·t) and each attack mode, then picks the hypothesis with the lowest accumulated squared residual ([Provan & Orizio](https://personales.upv.es/thinkmind/CENICS/CENICS_2017/cenics_2017_4_40_68006.html)). It gives a principled ten-way label and a likelihood that plugs straight into the Bayes layer. It is the most work and adds little over ranks 1–7 for a hackathon, so build it only if time remains.

## Prove the 99% against an attacker who adapts

The prototype's 99% comes from its own scenarios, and the field's track record warns against trusting that kind of figure. A survey of 609 industrial intrusion-detection papers found they **evaluate on only 1.3 datasets on average, with ambiguous metrics** ([SoK arXiv 2311.02929](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.02929)). Under point-adjust scoring, **a random anomaly score can reach state-of-the-art results** ([Kim et al. AAAI 2022](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/20680)). On SWaT, macro F1 falls from 85.44% for binary labels to 37.84% for behaviour classes ([arXiv 2606.30493](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.30493)). Urbina's central point is that an adaptive attacker who knows the threshold scores 0% detection by definition, so ROC curves are meaningless against it. The useful metric is **maximum undetected deviation versus expected time between false alarms**, E[T_fa] ([Urbina et al. 2016](https://users.soe.ucsc.edu/%7Ealacarde/papers/ccs16.pdf)).

A harness that would convince an OT judge has five parts. First, set thresholds only from healthy runs and report E[T_fa] from long healthy simulations; the current 40-run check is too short for this. Second, add a held-out adversarial set: a drift-shaped additive ramp, replay with fresh noise, a replay that also fakes motor feedback, an attacker spoofing both A and B, and a fault coinciding with an attack, the case Xue admits is ambiguous. Third, run a ramp-rate sweep to find the slowest ramp that evades every rule, and report the worst undetected overflow margin in centimetres; that is Urbina's metric, applied to this tank. Fourth, report a confusion matrix over all event types plus "normal", time-to-classify (a BATADAL scoring criterion; [Bath](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/battle-of-the-attack-detection-algorithms-disclosing-cyber-attack/)), an "always FAULT" baseline and a random baseline, and state explicitly that no point-adjust is used ([Garg et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.11428)). Fifth, randomize noise, leak size, onset time and tank area across seeds. For outside validation, HAI is an open 1 Hz physical-process dataset with normal-only training files ([HAI repo](https://github.com/icsdataset/hai)). The Morris/UAH power-system set is the only public set found that labels natural faults and attacks separately ([UAH](https://sites.google.com/a/uah.edu/tommy-morris-uah/ics-data-sets)). Avoid the older Morris water-tank set: its authors warn that attack-specific values let classifiers cheat.

## Conclusion

The research reframes what "Fault or Attack" is for. Its value is not a better anomaly detector, which vendors already sell. Its value is an explicit verdict built on consistency checks, a verdict that the field's own tools defer to humans with "operational issue or manipulation" wording. Its credibility will come from saying where that verdict is impossible. Replay and fabricated streams can be beaten decisively, with actuator coupling and a randomized-threshold watermark. An additive ramp on live data cannot be beaten from the sensor value alone. Showing judges that the detector catches noisy replay, bounds a stealthy ramp to a stated overflow margin, and returns UNCERTAIN with its reasons is a stronger story than 99%.

Ranks 1 through 4 plus the UNCERTAIN band fit in a day and close most of the practical gap, because they turn stateless checks into stateful ones and give the detector independent anchors to check against. The watermark is the one idea the literature has not yet built for on/off control, so a working, measured version on this tank would be a small novel contribution rather than a reimplementation.
